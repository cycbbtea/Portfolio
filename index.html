Project 01

Turning a Stalled Redesign Into a Fully Revamped User Experience

Avoided an unnecessary ground-up rebuild. 7 of 11 improvement areas prioritized for release instead of restarting.

Context

A flagship redesign stalled: more than half of enterprise customers stayed on the legacy experience, and several called the new one a barrier to renewal. Before a costly ground-up rebuild, the team needed to know what was broken and what was worth keeping.

My Role &amp; Team

Led the research end to end — scoping, recruitment, interviews, synthesis — with the Head of Product, two PMs, the Design Manager, and a senior designer.

Approach
  1. 1

    Aligned around the unknowns: A cross-functional workshop surfaced 20 documented assumptions.

  2. 2

    Phase 1 — Diagnosed the adoption problem: 18 interviews, 15 enterprise customers, 7+ industries — migrated vs. stalled accounts.

    Focused on the “why”: interviews over a survey — was this the right experience, not just a usable one?

  3. 3

    Phase 2 — Validated the redesign: 24 prototype sessions with 21 customers before Design committed.

Key Findings
  • Keep critical information visible and easy to scan: Tab switching forced analysts to rebuild context. The consolidated landing page and content tree became the architectural foundation.
  • Keep secondary actions within the incident context: Response-task creation moved into a sidebar; filters sit directly above the data table.
  • Protect analysts’ primary workspace: Four columns felt crowded on laptops. Work notes stayed right; the checklist moved central.
  • Design around recurring user mindsets: 5 patterns — including “Familiar beats clever” — became a reusable decision framework.
User Mindsets
Don’t make me work to reach my data
Familiar beats clever
One size doesn’t fit all analysts
Impact
  • Avoided an unnecessary ground-up rebuild: 7 of 11 improvement areas prioritized for release instead of restarting.
  • Addressed roughly half of the prior year’s design debt: Validation finalized a base design, confirmed 3 variations, and identified 6 refinements.
  • Re-engaged 5 enterprise customers: All had walked away; all showed interest in migrating back to the redesigned workspace.
  • Introduced a reusable research sequence: “Validate assumptions before fielding” was reused on other flagship initiatives.
  • Created a lasting decision framework: 5 user mindsets became the lens for evaluating future concepts.
Reflection
When adoption struggles, the answer is not more redesign — it is reducing uncertainty about what must change and what users already value. Adoption rarely breaks on one usability issue; it sits between workflow fit, familiarity, information architecture, and willingness to change.
Project 02

Defining the First Use Case for a Cybersecurity AI Agent

Informed the first AI-agent use case. An internally debated list became a user-grounded ranking of 3; Product took the top 2.

Context

The company moved quickly into AI agents with several hypothesized use cases and no user evidence. We needed to find where an agent creates real value in security managers’ daily work — and where human judgment must stay in control.

My Role &amp; Team

Led the study end to end — scoping, recruitment, interviews, synthesis — partnering with the PMs and designer driving AI-agent work for the business unit.

Approach
  1. 1

    Reframed the problem to two foundational questions: Who are security managers, and where do recurring challenges create agent opportunities?

  2. 2

    Conducted JTBD interviews: 4 managers leading global SOCs with 10+ analysts and 400–700 incidents a month.

  3. 3

    Prioritized opportunities: Ranked by consistency of need, effort reduced, and where human judgment must remain.

Key Findings
  • Explain why incident-response time deviates from target: Surface deviations and supporting evidence; leave interpretation to the manager.
  • Highlight likely bottlenecks behind slower response: Use assignment timing, reassignment, and follow-up duration for a first-pass diagnosis.
  • Support incident-quality and compliance reviews: Select cases, summarize notes, flag deviations — managers keep final judgment.
  • The same pattern held across all three: AI earned trust by reducing the investigation required before a decision — not by making the decision itself.
Shipped Features

Publicly shipped features that incorporated the research insights

Impact
  • Informed the first AI-agent use case: An internally debated list became a user-grounded ranking of 3; Product took the top 2.
  • Established a testable efficiency hypothesis: Estimated 1–2 hours saved per security manager per week.
  • Reduced risk before build: Avoided roughly 2 months of Product and Engineering effort on a lower-priority concept.
  • Defined a human-in-the-loop trust boundary: AI reduces investigative legwork; final operational and compliance calls stay human.
Reflection
Start with the work, not the technology: what users want to delegate matters more than what an agent can automate. Trust depends less on whether AI is accurate than on what users hand over — and what happens if the AI is wrong.
Project 03

Designing Trust, Control, and Transparency Into AI-Native Security Experiences

Shaped key interaction patterns in the shipped AI experience. Expandable AI reasoning, manual approval for consequential actions, feedback on rejected actions.

Context

As the company moved toward an AI-native vision, Design had proposed general AI interaction patterns. We needed to know whether they worked for security professionals, and how they should support trust and control when AI acts on a user’s behalf.

My Role &amp; Team

Owned the research for my business unit inside a cross-BU initiative — recruitment through synthesis — and presented with the team to the Product VP and Design VP.

Approach
  1. 1

    Reframed the research: From testing patterns in isolation to where AI can act, and how patterns fit mental models.

  2. 2

    Mapped what we knew vs. needed to validate: Separated tasks users would delegate, review, or keep.

  3. 3

    Validated the use cases first: 2 exploratory sessions to clarify assumptions before prototypes.

  4. 4

    Tested the interactions: 5 concept-testing sessions on reasoning, trust, control, and oversight.

Key Findings

Four mechanisms for strengthening trust, transparency, and user control:

  • Make AI reasoning available without overwhelming the workflow: Access on demand, not always on — an expandable view balanced transparency with focus.
  • Preserve human control over consequential actions: Routine work can be delegated; user-facing actions need approval and feedback.
  • Keep AI and human actions in one operational history: A unified timeline supports reconstruction, real-time collaboration, and audits.
  • Make AI-generated information traceable: Show the references behind outputs and separate AI interpretation from system fact.
Impact
  • Shaped key interaction patterns in the shipped AI experience: Expandable AI reasoning, manual approval for consequential actions, feedback on rejected actions.
  • Improved visibility and accountability: A unified timeline of AI and human activity now supports collaboration and audits.
  • Created reusable AI design guidance: A knowledge base referenced by teams in other business units.
Reflection
Trust was not earned by intelligent-looking output. It came from visible reasoning, control over consequential actions, clear provenance, and accountability for what both sides did. UXR’s job is evaluating system behavior and the human–AI relationship, not just usability.
Project 04

Turning Implementation Barriers Into Cross-Functional Initiatives

Turned a broad concern into a shared roadmap. Short-, medium-, and long-term recommendations for five functions.

Context

Adoption was a top priority and implementation the critical lever. Customers stalled on foundational features, and real-world adoption sat below the 50% internal target across a portfolio of 15+ products.

My Role &amp; Team

Ran the study with another researcher — literature review plus interviews with 5 customers, 9 implementation partners, and 2 internal teams — alongside product leadership, Partner Excellence, and Customer Success.

Approach
  1. 1

    Scoped before fielding: Reviewed implementation studies from other business units to isolate open questions.

  2. 2

    Adapted an existing framework: Focused the study on the hypotheses leadership most needed tested.

  3. 3

    Interviewed the people doing the work: 5 customers and 9 implementation partners — administrators, implementers, and solution architects — plus 2 internal teams.

  4. 4

    Identified why barriers existed: Interviews traced the root causes, the workarounds implementers built, and the support they expected.

Key Findings
  1. 1Before implementation

    Set expectations before implementation: Unclear tiers and constraints drove customization and slow time to value; enablement arrived too late.

  2. 2During implementation

    Make guidance findable and actionable: Good resources were scattered; customers needed a centralized, role-specific path.

  3. 3When it gets complex

    Reduce friction when implementation becomes complex: Integrations, configuration limits, and slow support cases delayed every role.

Impact
  • Turned a broad concern into a shared roadmap: Short-, medium-, and long-term recommendations for five functions.
  • Aligned 5 functions around shared evidence: A common view of barriers and clearer ownership of improvements.
  • Strengthened the Resource Library as a central hub: Outbound PMs refined it into a destination for updates, announcements, FAQs, and quick-start guidance.
  • Prompted documentation centralization: The Documentation team consolidated scattered resources across the security product portfolio.
Reflection
Enterprise experience does not begin and end in the interface — expectations, onboarding, documentation, partners, and support shape it too. And handoff is the start of a measurement loop, not the end of the research.
